Genres and voice

Status: LIVE — you leave able to decide what a page is, scaffold it, write it so it reads like the rest of its kind, and say in its first line whether it is still true.

§1 · Decide what the page is

Before the first sentence, decide what kind of page you are writing, because the answer decides where the file goes, how it is built and how it should read. That kind is the page's genre. There are nine in the core:

GenreWrite one when you have…It lands at
noteone claim and its reason, in a few paragraphscontent/notes/<slug>.html
concepta term other pages keep needing, pinned down oncecontent/concepts/<slug>/index.html
entrya dense one-page read: a deep dive, a reading of a papercontent/entries/<slug>/index.html
chapterone step of a sequence someone reads in ordercontent/books/<book>/NN-name.html
booka sequence of chapters that needs a mapcontent/books/<book>/index.html
huba cluster of pages someone arriving cold must find their way throughcontent/hubs/<slug>.html
projecta piece of work whose state changes, datedcontent/projects/<slug>/index.html
papera paper to frame: abstract, submission state, where the source iscontent/papers/<slug>/index.html
relatedother people's work on your question, summarised neutrallycontent/related/<slug>.html

Choose by what the page is, not by how long it will be. A page explaining your club's counting method to a new member is an entry if it stands alone, and a chapter if it is one step in a sequence they read in order. Length follows from the choice.

Check-yourself: you want a page listing what other clubs have published on counting methods, and what you make of it. Which genre?
Related: their work in a neutral table, one row per work, and what you make of it in a separate, clearly marked section at the end.

§2 · ckit new and the voice card

Never start a page from a blank file. ckit new <genre> <slug> copies the genre's skeleton to where the genre says it lives, and prints the genre's voice card:

$ ckit new note swifts-follow-weather --title "Swifts follow the weather"
created content/notes/swifts-follow-weather.html
note — assertive, unhedged, one claim
  reader:    knows the domain
  forbidden: sections, scaffolding, growing past a few paragraphs — promote to an entry
  checked:   status=True, max_words=400, no_h2=True

The card has four lines, and each tells you something different. The first is the register: how the page sounds. Reader is who you are writing for, and so what you may assume. Forbidden is the moves that make a page stop being this genre. Checked is the part of all that the gate enforces; everything else is on you.

Slugs are lowercase words joined by dashes. A chapter's slug is its book and its numbered name, and the book has to exist first:

$ ckit new chapter field-guide/03-gulls
no book at content/books/field-guide — `ckit new book field-guide` first

ckit genres lists every genre the library knows, including any the library adds itself.

Check-yourself: which line of the voice card will the gate never check for you?
The register and the reader. The gate checks the proxies listed under checked (a word bound, no sections, a status line); whether the page sounds assertive and suits a reader who knows the domain is judged by whoever reads it.

§3 · Write in the voice

Here is one subject, the flyway swifts use to reach the reservoir, written for two genres:

As a note

Watch the flyway, not the calendar. Swifts reach us when the weather all along their route lets them, so a cold week over Spain means a late week here, whatever the date says last year.

One claim, stated as true, with its reason in the same breath. No sections, no "in this note".

As a concept

FLYWAY — A flyway is the set of routes a population of migratory birds uses between its breeding and wintering grounds, together with the stopover sites along them.

Impersonal and timeless. It would be as true in ten years, and it says nothing about this spring.

Each genre also has moves that break it. A note that hedges ("it may perhaps be that…") or grows headings has stopped being a note. A concept whose definition cites a running count ("the flyway currently carries about 400 swifts a day") will be wrong next week; the count can go below the definition, cited, but never inside it. A hub that explains instead of pointing is an entry in disguise. A chapter that links to a later chapter breaks the promise that the reader needs only what came before; when a look ahead is deliberate, the link carries data-fwd to say so.

Every genre's register, reader and forbidden moves are written out in kit/genres/GENRES.md. Read the entry for your genre before you write, not after.

Check-yourself: a concept's definition says "the reservoir flyway currently carries about 400 swifts a day". What is wrong, and where does the number go?
The definition depends on a running count, so it stops being true when the count changes. Define the term without it; if the count matters, give it below the definition with its source, the line of the log it came from.

§4 · Status in the first line

Every page's first <p class="sub"> says whether the page is still true, in one of six words. The gate checks that the word is there:

content/notes/swifts-follow-weather.html: no status in the opening line — the first <p class="sub"> must say one of LIVE · HISTORICAL · PARKED · RETIRED · FROZEN · DRAFT

Which word is right is your call. These are the conventions:

StatusTells the reader
LIVEmaintained, and true now
DRAFTbeing written; do not rely on it yet
PARKEDpaused on purpose; true as of when it stopped, and may resume
FROZENfixed at a moment on purpose, such as a paper at submission, and not revised
HISTORICALwas true; kept as an account of how things were
RETIREDno longer in use; kept so links still resolve, and pointing at what replaced it
Check-yourself: last year's survey method was replaced this spring, but old pages still link to its how-to. What status does the how-to get?
RETIRED, with a link in its first line to the page that replaced it. The old links keep working, and nobody following one mistakes the old method for the current one.

§5 · Promotion, and defining once

Pages change kind as they grow, and the lint tells you when. A note that has sprouted sections says so:

content/notes/swifts-follow-weather.html: note has <h2> sections — a note is one claim; promote it

Promote it: move it to content/entries/<slug>/index.html and rewrite it in an entry's register. The usual path is note, then entry, then chapter, as the scope grows from a claim to an argument to a step in a course. Do not stretch a page to fit a genre it has outgrown.

Terms get the same treatment. The second time a term needs explaining anywhere in the library, it becomes a concept: its own page, with a blockquote.defn that states it once. Every other mention becomes a link to it:

<a class="defn-link" href="/content/concepts/flyway/">flyway</a>

The shell shows the definition when a reader hovers the link, the way this book's own skeleton link does. That is why a concept's definition has to make sense on its own, with no page around it.

Check-yourself: two notes each spend a paragraph explaining what a stopover site is. What do you do?
Write content/concepts/stopover-site/index.html with one definition of record, then replace both paragraphs with a defn-link to it. The explanation now lives in one place, and a correction there reaches every page.
